01
What the game is

Ten minutes, ten swaps, a hundred letters

Wottle asks one thing of a player every round: find, among a hundred letters, the single swap that makes the most valuable word, and make it before the other player does. Everything else on the screen exists either to support that act or to record its result. That is the test every element has to pass.

A match is ten rounds. Each player has one ten-minute clock for the whole match; it runs while their move for the round is open and stops when they submit, so time spent on this round is taken from the rounds to come. The game rests on three skills and two resources.

Skill · Scanning

Word-search perception. The eye runs rows and columns looking for near-words. The field must be a clean text to read, not a set of objects to inspect.

Skill · Valuation

Letter points (Krafla table, Þ 7, X 10) plus (length−2)×5 plus a combo bonus. Valuation happens after a candidate is found, never while scanning.

Skill · Timing

The first submission wins coordinate conflicts and freezes tiles instantly, denying them to the opponent. Speed is rewarded twice: it takes letters from the opponent and it banks clock for later rounds.

Resource · Time

One 10:00 clock per player, running only while your move is open. A long think now is borrowed from the rounds to come, and the opponent's clock tells you whether they can afford one.

Resource · Territory

Scored letters freeze in the scorer's colour. Territory shrinks the field, feeds the tiebreak, and, by rule §3.5a, always comes in whole words of three or more.

Rhythm · A round

Set → think → commit → reveal → settle. Five beats, ten times. The panel's job is to make each beat unmistakable with exactly one signal.

Principles derived from the rules

  1. The field is the game. Nothing covers it, nothing competes with it, and nothing on it that is not a letter or a state of a letter. What frames it above and below belongs to the two players and to nothing else.
  2. Show words, not tiles. Territory is stored per tile but earned per word. Draw the word.
  3. Two budgets, one scale. Both clocks are match-long budgets. Draw them as two lengths on one axis so who can afford to think is a glance, and keep the numerals in the ledger.
  4. One ledger. Every number about the match (scores, rounds, words, territory) lives in one scorecard, in one place, in one register.
  5. Colour is identity. Two inks belong to the two seats: you are always teal, the opponent always coral. The system itself speaks only in ink and paper; alarm is weight and motion, not a third hue.
  6. Each beat has one signal. A round announcement, a lock banner, a ring flash, a popup and a list update for the same event is five signals for one fact.
  7. Teach once, then leave. Rules are for the first match and a help key, not for the panel of every match.

04
Proposal

Field, two bars, ledger

Everything the panel currently does is re-homed into three places, arranged as online chess arranges them. The field is the hundred letters and the state of each letter. Two player bars frame it, the opponent's above and yours below, each carrying only what belongs to that player: name, rating, clock and total, with the bar's edge nearest the field serving as that player's clock lane. The ledger beside them holds what belongs to the match: round, words, territory and the two quiet controls. There are no cards, no top bar, no overlays and no third colour. Opponent above, self below is the arrangement every board-game player already knows; the eye finds the opponent's time exactly where it finds the opponent.

Identity: set in type, marked in two inks

Warm Editorial borrows its look from magazines. The proposal takes its look from the game's own material: a printed word-search page, set in a slab serif so Þ, Ð, Æ and Ö carry weight, marked up by two players with two inks. Paper and ink are the system; coral and teal are the players. No gradients, no shadows, no rounded cards, no italic display type.

Inks belong to seats, not to accounts: you are always teal, the opponent always coral, on every device and in every match, so your own words are the same colour every time you play. Inks are used at full strength for letters and at 14% as the tint of a settled word. There is no alarm colour: urgency is thickness, size and blink in ink. Coral and teal are separable for the common colour-vision deficiencies and also differ in lightness, so runs read apart in greyscale.

The field

A ruled grid of one hundred capitals in ink on paper, framed above and below by the two player bars and by nothing else: no labels, no hint line. Cells are flat; the rules between them are a hairline; the frame is 1.5px ink. Each cell carries its Krafla value as a small mono numeral tucked into the top-right corner, which the word bands are inset to clear, so it can be read when a player is comparing two candidate swaps and ignored when they are not. On a scored letter the numeral takes the scorer's ink, so the word and its values read as one mark. Eight things can happen to a letter, and each has one mark.
{{ c.letter }}
Settled words are runs

One band per scored word: a 14% tint of the scorer's ink with square ends aligned to the cell grid. A fine chevron in that ink, opened to about 150°, sits on the band's leading edge and gives the reading direction; the end of the word needs no mark, because the band simply stops. A word may be read in any of the four directions, and the chevron sits at the end where reading begins: on the right, pointing left, for a right-to-left word; at the bottom, pointing up, for one read upwards. A run that is a valid word both ways (FÁR and RÁF, LÁN and NÁL) scores twice and carries a chevron at each end. Start and direction are read in a glance, and the last letter looks like every other letter in the word. A scored word is always the whole run: it may not abut another scored word in its own line unless the combined run is itself a word (BORÐA and GILT are words, BORÐAGILT is not, so GILT laid against BORÐA scores nothing). Two bands therefore never touch end to end, and the field stays readable as words. The band is inset to 60% of the cell across its short axis, which leaves the top of every cell — the numeral gutter — on clean paper, so a value glyph never crosses a band edge. A crossing shows both bands; the shared letter is set in ink black. Territory reads as words, which is what it is. Partial freezes (the 24-tile floor, rule §6) draw the run only over the frozen letters.

Picked, previewed, pinned

A picked letter turns your ink, grows 8% and gets an ink square ring; its value goes to full-strength ink. Touching a second letter previews the exchange in place, and the ledger's hint line prints the total of the word the preview makes, so the choice between two candidate swaps is a comparison of two numbers rather than mental arithmetic. Once committed, both letters are pinned: your ink, dashed ring, no fill. The opponent's played letters pin in their ink the moment they play, as rule §2 broadcasts them.

The clocks

Each player bar's edge nearest the field is that player's clock lane: as wide as the field, on one scale, full width being the 10:00 each started with. The opponent's lane closes the top bar, yours opens the bottom bar, so the two lanes bracket the field with a shared left origin and who can afford to think is read as a difference in length, not by subtracting two numerals. A running lane drains; a stopped lane holds, so “waiting” is your lane standing still while the opponent's moves. The mm:ss stands at the centre of the bar, in ink while running and muted when stopped. Under 1:00 a lane doubles in thickness and blinks at 1 Hz; under reduced motion it holds solid. This keeps O-59's intent without five tones or two renderings.

The ledger

A scorecard with ten rows, one per round, and two columns, one per player. It is the only place a number appears. It holds what belongs to the match rather than to a player: the round count, one row per round with each player's words and round total, the live row showing each player's state (picking, played, then the words), territory, the one-line hint for the current state, and the two quiet controls (rules, menu). It runs the full height of the stack, from the top of the opponent's bar to the bottom of yours, and the ten round rows share that height equally, so each row has room for three lines of words before anything needs to collapse. Because the ledger is a table and not a stack of cards, it also serves as round history, post-game recap and spectator view without a second component.

  • A round can score up to eight readings per player (across and down at each swapped letter, each run possibly valid in both directions). The cell lists them in play order separated by a middle dot and wraps to a second line when it must, with the round total pinned at the right; per-word points show on hover or tap, when the field also lights that word's band. Rows grow only as needed. Only if a row would overflow its three lines do rounds older than the last three collapse to totals, so ten rows always fit beside the field without scrolling.
  • The score delta is not a popup. At the reveal, the word and its points are written into the live row while the total in the header counts up over 400 ms. The eye is already there.
  • Combo and duplicate outcomes appear as suffixes in the row: +18 · +2 combo, SKÁL · again · 0.
  • Territory is a two-ink bar under the table (10 · 81 free · 9). The tiebreak is visible all match without a card.
  • Elo, mode and format belong to the pre-match screen and the ledger's caption line, not the HUD.
  • Hover or tap a row and the field highlights those runs; the same gesture drives the post-game recap.

06
Layouts

The field always fits without scrolling

The layout rule is a single constraint: the field is the largest square that fits the viewport after the two player bars are placed above and below it. On wide screens the full ledger sits beside the stack; on narrow screens it collapses to its live row beneath your bar, and the rest pulls up. There is no third breakpoint and no sticky navigation during a match.

Desktop ≥ 1100

Opponent bar, field, your bar stacked left; ledger 340px right, 56px gutter. The field is min(viewport height − 2 bars − 48px, 720px) square, so tiles run 50–70px.

Laptop 900–1100

Same arrangement; the ledger narrows to 260px and drops word points to a hover. The field keeps priority: on a 13-inch display at 100% zoom it is 560px, never below the fold. Today's layout budgets 48px of chrome (board.css) but spends about 190px.

Phone landscape

Bars shrink to one line each and the field fills the remaining height; the ledger shows the round count and live row only, with the history behind a tap.

Phone portrait

Opponent bar, full-width field, your bar, live row: the desktop arrangement unchanged, with the ledger folded to its live row. The two compact bars with two clock pills, two scores and two "R4" labels become two bars whose inner edges are the clocks.

07
Interaction spec

Pick, preview, commit

Today the second tap submits an irrevocable move. The proposal inserts one beat: the second tap previews the exchange in place, and the player commits by tapping either previewed letter again (or Enter). The preview lets the player read the word they are about to make, which is the check most mis-taps fail. The cost is one tap; the alternative is a lost round. An "instant commit" setting can remove the beat for players who want it.

tap A
Pick. Letter turns your ink, grows 8%, ink square ring, value shown. Sound: tile-select.
tap B
Preview. The two letters exchange places in 150 ms; both carry a dotted ink ring, and the ledger's hint line prints the word total. Nothing is sent. Any new run through A or B is not hinted; that would be a spoiler.
tap A or B again · Enter
Commit. Rings turn dashed (pinned), your clock lane stops, ledger row writes played. Sound: valid-swap, haptic.
tap elsewhere · Esc · tap A
Cancel. Preview reverses; pick clears. No sound.
drag A → B (pointer)
Same as tap A, tap B: a preview, not a commit. Fulfils PRD §1.3 desktop drag without a separate path.
tap a frozen or pinned letter
Letter shakes 300 ms in its own ink; the live row reads frozen · Kári R2 for 2 s. No red, no blue: an error is a fact about the board, not a colour.
long-press · hover 600 ms
Highlight every letter of the same value on the field, for players hunting a high-value swap. The full Krafla table lives behind the ledger's ?.
opponent plays
Their two letters pin in their ink at once (rule §2). If one of them was your pick, your pick clears and the live row says so, as spec 042 FR-004 already requires.
keyboard
Arrows move a focus ring in ink, Space picks or previews, Enter commits. Coordinates stay in the accessible name of each cell for screen readers; they are no longer printed.
first match
Three sentences before round 1, over a live field, dismissed by the first pick: Swap two letters. Words of three or more score and freeze in your ink. Ten rounds; your clock holds ten minutes for all of them. Never shown again; ? in the ledger foot reopens it.

Open questions for the team

  • O-59 asked for prominent timers and got two 36px clocks. The lanes are larger by area than today's clocks and bracket the board as the inner edges of the player bars, with the mm:ss at each bar's centre. Is that prominent enough for the people who filed O-59?
  • The commit beat adds roughly 300 ms to every move in a game where the first submission wins conflicts. Ship it default-on with an instant-commit setting, or default-off?
  • Partial freezes can leave a run half frozen. Draw the run over frozen letters only (proposed), or over the whole word with unfrozen letters hollow?
  • Coral/teal are proposed inks; the brand ochre can take the opponent's seat if the brand needs it on the board, provided it stops meaning anything else.
09
The rest of the game

One room: the board never leaves

Outside the match, a player wants two things: the next match in as few steps as possible, and to know how they are doing. Today that costs five pages (landing, lobby, matchmaking, match, summary) in three visual registers, with the board visible on one of them. The proposal is a single room. The field, the two bars and the ledger are on screen from the moment a person arrives to the moment they leave; the lobby, the queue, the match and the result are states of those three objects, not pages. Nothing has to be learned twice, and the letters are always there to be looked at.

  • The opponent's bar is the door. Empty, it holds the one action: play ranked. Searching, it counts the wait. Found, it writes the opponent's name in. The lobby needs no hero and no Play card.
  • The field is the waiting room. In the lobby it is a warm-up field: swap letters, see values and word totals, nobody keeps score. In the queue the next match's letters land in reading order; when the last one lands, the opponent is usually there. No spinner, no ring.
  • The ledger is the record. In the lobby it lists who is here and your last matches. In the match it is the scorecard. Afterwards it is the result, with the field frozen beside it so the match can be read back. On the profile it is the same table over a longer time.
  • A name is the only account. The landing screen is the lobby with the bottom bar asking for your name. Type it, press play, you are in the queue.

Open questions for the loop

  • Setting the field in the queue previews the real match letters. If both players see them before round 1, the pre-scan is symmetric; if the queue is long, should the letters reshuffle when the opponent arrives?
  • The warm-up field never keeps score. Should it, as a solo mode with a personal best, or would that pull players out of the ranked loop?
  • What happens when a clock reaches 0:00 is not in the rules document read for this audit. The bar can carry any answer (the lane empties and the sub-line states the consequence), but the rule has to be decided first.
  • Challenges from the directory are proposed as unranked. Is a ranked challenge wanted, and if so, does it need a consent step in the challenged player's bar?
